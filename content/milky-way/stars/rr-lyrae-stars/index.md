## Overview

:::{sidebar} Variable Stars
A variable star is a star whose brightness changes over time. Variations may be caused by processes inherent to the star itself, such as starspots or a change in a star's equilibrium. Or, variations in brightness may be due to another body passing in front of the star from our perspective, like an eclipsing binary star system.
:::

RR Lyrae variable stars are pulsating stars that we use as so-called standard candles to derive distances to objects within and beyond the Milky Way. In contrast to [Cepheid stars](/content/milky-way/stars/cepheid-stars/index), RR Lyrae stars are older, less luminous than the sun, and found in the Galaxy's halo. They have shorter periods than Cepheids, and are often found in [globular star clusters](/content/milky-way/star-clusters/globular-clusters/index).

Because they are dimmer than Cepheids, RR Lyrae stars were not discovered until the 1890s. Initially called "cluster variables" because they were often seen in globular star clusters, these stars were better understood and classified in the first few decades of the twentieth century.



:::{seealso}
For an explanation of the pulsation mechanism that causes variability in these stars, see [Why Some Stars Vary in Brightness](content--milky-way--stars--cepheid-stars).
:::



## Distribution of RR Lyrae Stars

RR Lyrae stars are dimmer, older stars, so they tend to be found in the Galactic halo, the spherical distrubution of stars that encompass the disk of the Milky Way. This is consistent with their correlation with globular clusters, which are clusters of old stars distributed spherically around the Milky Way.



## Determining Distances with RR Lyrae Stars

RR Lyrae stars are not as simple as Cepheid stars in their period-luminosity relationship. Such a relationship is only visible in infrared light, but astronomers more often use a period-color relationship for these stars. RR Lyraes also suffer from being in dense globular clusters, so their brightness is often blended with neighboring stars in the field of view, making their use as standard candles slighty more muddied. 

However, we know of many standalone RR Lyrae stars that are not in crowded globular clusters. In the past few decades, astronomers have used powerful telescopes, including Hubble, Kepler, Gaia, and others, to measure accurate brightnesses and, therefore, get accurate distances to these stars. Their place among the distance ladder, upon which rests our understanding of cosmic distances, is secure.
