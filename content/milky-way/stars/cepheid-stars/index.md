## Overview

:::{sidebar} Variable Stars
A variable star is a star whose brightness changes over time. Variations may be caused by processes inherent to the star itself, such as starspots or a change in a star's equilibrium. Or, variations in brightness may be due to another body passing in front of the star from our perspective, like an eclipsing binary star system.
:::

Cepheid stars are variable stars that provide an important benchmark for determining cosmic distances. The name *Cepheid* is a reference to the constellation Cepheus, where the Cephied variable star Delta Cephei is located. Delta Cephei was one of the first of these variable stars to be discovered in the 1780s. 

By the end of the nineteenth century, a few dozen Cepheid stars were known to astronomers. These stars are giants and supergiants, so they are not in static equilibrium and have expanded off the {term}`main sequence`. They are more massive than the Sun, and far more luminous.





## Why Some Stars Vary in Brightness

Cepheid stars, RR Lyrae stars, and other variable stars attribute their variability in brightness to a pulsation mechanism that occurs in their stellar atmosphere. The opacity of the atmosphere---that is, how transparent the atmosphere is to electromagnetic radiation, or how much light can escape---drives this pulsation cycle.

In a normal star, equilibrium is maintained between the gravitational compression of the atmosphere, which results in an increase in temperature and desity and, hence, a decrease in opacity which allows more light to escape. This defines the {term}`main sequence` phase of a star, where gravitational collapse and outward radiation pressure keep the temperature in balance and the light from the star is output at a constant rate.

In a pulsating variable star, a layer of the atmosphere moves inward causing it to become more dense and opaque to light. This causes the heat and pressure to build up because less energy escapes, causing the star to dim. This increased pressure pushes the layer back out, causing the opacity to decrease and more light to escape. This is when the star is at its maximum brightness.

These pulsating variable stars are not in equilibrium, but in a stable cycle of brightening, then dimming, due to these atmospheric pulsations. The Cepheid namesake, Delti Cephei, ranges in magnitude from 3.48--4.37 over 5.37 days, almost one magnitude in difference in its pulsation cycle. RR Lyrae, the eponym for the RR Lyrae class of variable stars, has a range in brightness of 7.06--8.12 over 13.5 hours.




## Cepheids as Our First Standard Candle

In astrophysics, the term *standard candle* refers to an object with a known intrinsic brightness. This brightness can be used to determine the object's distance. Cepheids played a critical role in our understanding of the cosmic distance ladder, once we discovered the underlying mechanism that makes them standard candles.

This discovery came in 1908 by Henrietta Swan Leavitt (1868--1921) at the Harvard College Observartory. Working as a "human computer," she discovered a relationship between the star's luminosity and its period of variability. She was looking at Cepheid stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) from photographic plates taken at a Harvard Observatory in Peru. She identified Cepheids in the SMC and noticed that the brighter variables had longer periods. This resulted in a relationship between the period of the variable star's brightness (dim to bright to dim cycle) and its luminosity. This period-luminosity relation is now called Leavitt's Law.

She approximated the distance to the Cepheids as the same, since they are all within the SMC, though she did not know their actual distance. She also understood that the apparent magnitude of each star, the brightness as we see the star in the sky, was equivalent to the star's absolute magnitude, its intrinsic brightness, with an offset related to the, as yet unknown, distance to the star.

One year after her work, a parallax to a Cepheid star was obtained by Danish astronomer Ejnar Hertzsprung (1873--1967), so the distances to all Cepheids could be calibrated. This was the last key to making Cepheids the first standard candles in astronomy. He then calculated the distance to Leavett's Cepheids in the SMC and determined the distance to dwarf galaxy to be about 30,000 light years. This was an enormous underestimation---we now know the true distance to be roughly 200,000 light years---but it remained a technique that, upon refinement and better data, was used to determine that our galaxy was not alone. Edwin Hubble (1889--1953) measured Cepheids in what was then known as the Andromeda Nebula to establish that, in fact, it was not a member of the Milky Way. This led to our understanding that the Milky Way was not the entirety of the universe, and that Andromeda was, indeed, an entire galaxy unto itself.
